# Phase 1 - Literature Review

**Project:** Predicting Highly Cited Research Papers - Which Factors Drive Citation Impact, and Can Textual Features Improve Prediction?
**Course:** CSE437: Data Science

## 1 Abstract

Predicting the citation impact of research papers is challenging, as citation accumulation depends on an interplay of internal research quality and external contextual factors. While extensive research has utilized text embeddings, Large Language Models (LLMs), and econometric models to forecast citation counts, current literature remains fragmented. Existing studies often prioritize either content-based signals, such as abstract semantics and methodological novelty, or non-content bibliometric indicators, including author prestige, journal impact factors, collaboration networks, and reference-list characteristics. This review synthesizes evidence from twelve key studies to identify the primary determinants of citation success, analyzing how factors such as topic growth, interdisciplinary reach, and early citation trajectories influence long-term visibility. The review reveals that although LLMs and machine-learning algorithms are increasingly effective at predicting citation impact from textual content alone, the "Matthew effect" (the tendency of prestigious authors and journals to garner disproportionate citations) remains a dominant influence. Furthermore, the findings suggest that citation prediction is highly domain-specific, with models often struggling to generalize across diverse fields. This review highlights a critical gap in the field: the need for integrated models that simultaneously account for scientific content, bibliometric exposure, and temporal dynamics, evaluated prospectively, to provide a more holistic and interpretable understanding of what drives citation impact in the modern scholarly ecosystem.

## 2 Literature Review

Citations accumulate according to a complex distribution that is highly skewed by factors which are internal to the quality of the research contribution and external ones, such as author and journal prestige, author collaboration networks, open access versus non-open-access publication, popularity of the research topic, and early discoverability. Predicting future citations accurately could be useful for numerous applications, such as post-publication review, grant and funding decisions, and science discovery in general. Work over the last decade has fallen into two categories. Some papers view the text of a paper as the main signal and use paper embeddings or large language models to predict citation counts. Others model citation counts as a function of observable bibliometric and network properties using regression or machine learning informed by features derived from large-scale scholarly databases.

While progress has been made, most prior work focuses on one particular field of study, few works combine signals from both content and non-content sources, and even fewer study these relationships across fields with different citation practices. We analyzed the literature across three foci that connect to these perspectives: (i) content-based prediction using embeddings and large language models, (ii) econometric and multifactor models that focus on non-content determinants, and (iii) broader comparative and sequential studies that help clarify the roles of interdisciplinarity, temporal effects, reference-list behaviors, and early citation influence.

These three streams map directly onto the design of this project: we evaluate content-only models, bibliometric-only models, and an integrated hybrid model, all under a strict temporal (train-past, test-future) protocol.

### 2.1 Vital Jr. et al. (2024): Prediction of Citation Impacts via Text Embeddings and GPT

Vital Jr. et al.'s study evaluates whether the content of a paper itself, independent of institution, affiliation, or author reputation, can predict whether it will be among the top 20% most cited papers within its journal. For 40,000 papers published in ACS Applied Materials and Interfaces from 2012-2022, abstracts and full texts were embedded with five methods (InferSent, GPT-3.5 text-embedding-ada-002, TF-IDF plus SVD, Sentence-BERT, and Universal Sentence Encoder), two target definitions were used (Yearly Citation Count, YCC, and Accumulative Citation Count, ACC), and classifiers were trained on four algorithms (Random Forest, Regression, XGBoost, kNN, and Logistic Regression). While Random Forest models trained on GPT embedding vectors performed best overall at 80% accuracy on cumulative citation count (ACC), TF-IDF embeddings came in a close second, suggesting that "keywords/topics" provide a good deal of predictive signal even compared with dense embeddings. The authors found that full-text processing led to marginally better performance than abstracts alone; models also performed worse on imbalanced ("realistic") data than on balanced data. Robustness checks across the top 10%, 20%, 30%, and 40% thresholds show that performance remains stable on balanced datasets.

Because the dataset spans a single journal, even the convincing finding that "the abstract is almost enough" may not hold in other disciplines. Citation count is treated here as a function of content features exclusively; institution and author metadata are omitted from the analysis. Consequently, this paper describes the predictability of citations based on content only, not the citation formation process as a whole. It also lacks interpretability regarding whether predictors inherit topical or stylistic biases; deployed uncritically, such models risk undervaluing unconventional methodologies or lesser-known authors.

### 2.2 Dorta-González & Gómez-Déniz (2025): A Two-Stage Model for Factors Influencing Citation Counts

Dorta-González and Gómez-Déniz take an econometric approach in which citation count is modeled with negative binomial regression calibrated for overdispersion in the outcome variable (sample variance exceeds sample mean, violating the assumption of the Poisson distribution), together with a "hurdle" component distinguishing papers that received zero citations from those that did not: a two-stage Hurdle Negative Binomial (HNB) regression. Modeling 43,190 Economics and Business articles published from 2014-2021, after merging scraped data from The Lens with SCImago Journal Rank, Altmetric, and the ABDC journal quality list compiled by the Australian Business Deans Council, they find that co-authorship collaboration and research funding predict higher citation counts and lower odds of receiving zero citations; all three OA types (green, bronze, and hybrid) correlate with higher citation counts, though the hybrid OA effect is smaller, and contrary to expectations bronze OA also predicts greater odds of zero citations; news and blog mentions lead to more citations; patents negatively affect citation count. HNB significantly outperforms both plain Poisson regression and ordinary Negative Binomial regression on AIC, lending support to the authors' hypothesis that citation accumulation behaves like a two-stage process.

The dataset is limited to a single academic field. Economics and Business is unique in many ways, with more conservative publication practices, slower open-access uptake, and quicker turnover of literature than most other academic fields. In addition, author count is only a proxy for collaboration quality and may mask diminishing returns inside very large teams.

### 2.3 Zhu et al. (2025): A Multidomain Comparative Analysis on Scientific Papers' Citation Impact

Zhu et al. conducted a cross-disciplinary analysis in three fields - mathematics, computer science, and management (roughly 33k-41k papers each) - using journal rosters from ACM, AMS, and UTD24. Using XGBoost, Gradient Boosting Decision Tree models, and Random Forest prediction models, as well as Ordinary Least Squares and Quantile Regression models, they rank the relative importance of a variety of non-content factors: mean prior citation count of authors, author count, country collaboration count, keyword count, reference count, page count, journal impact factor, and days until first citation.

Author prestige and reference count are the most important predictors of citation count in all models, datasets, and evaluation metrics. Author prestige shows particularly strong performance at higher citation quantiles, suggesting that "excess citations" accrue to papers by prestigious authors more than to others. Interestingly, author count and country count negatively predict citation counts in mathematics and CS, whereas categorical breadth is a positive predictor in management papers.

The authors interpret the dominance of prestige as quantitative evidence of citation inequality (the Matthew effect): high-prestige authors enjoy advantages through visibility, editorial preference, and resource concentration. However, the paper studies only non-content factors; it cannot provide information about what a paper actually says. The authors themselves admit that heavy dependence of academic citations on author reputation and journal IFs suggests an uneven distribution of resources in academia that further aggravates citation inequality. This means such models cannot be used to surface high-quality work from low-prestige sources.

### 2.4 Sjögårde and Didegah (2022): Topic Growth as a Citation Impact Factor

Sjögårde and Didegah (2022) investigated whether the growth rate of a research topic is associated with the citation impact of publications within that topic. The authors proposed topic growth as a new citation-impact factor and analyzed more than 4,000 topics across eight broad research disciplines using Web of Science data. Research topics were constructed through community detection in a citation network, after which a two-part regression approach was applied. The first part used quantile regression to estimate the relationship between topic growth and citation counts among publications receiving more than three citations, while the second part used logistic regression to examine the probability of being lowly cited versus moderately or highly cited. Journal impact factor, number of authors, and number of references were included as control variables. The results showed a clear citation advantage for publications belonging to fast-growing topics compared with slow-growing or declining topics across all eight disciplines. The positive effect of topic growth also became stronger at higher citation quantiles, indicating that topic growth may be particularly relevant for highly cited publications. The authors suggest that a growing research topic creates a larger pool of subsequent publications that can cite earlier work, thereby increasing the opportunity for citation accumulation. However, the study did not control for several other potentially important factors, including author prestige, field impact, and open-access status. Furthermore, the authors note that the results cannot necessarily be generalized to all disciplines, and the observed relationship represents an association rather than proof that topic growth directly causes higher citation counts.

### 2.5 Ye et al. (2026): Prediction of Highly Cited Papers with Large Language Models

Ye et al. note that citation accumulation is highly skewed and slow, and that traditional predictors require extensive feature engineering and may miss semantic content. Instead of using embeddings as handcrafted numerical inputs to train a bespoke classifier, the authors used general-purpose LLMs - ChatGPT-4o mini, DeepSeek V3, DeepSeek R1, and Gemini 2.0 Flash - prompted to directly output predictions, driven entirely through structured prompt design rather than fine-tuning. The LLMs are evaluated against three criteria: (i) methodological innovation, (ii) expected long-term impact, and (iii) importance of the scientific problem. The LLMs are prompted to predict whether a statistics publication will become highly cited given only information available at publication time: keywords, abstract, title, and minimal metadata. The takeaway is that LLMs can identify meaningful predictors of long-term citation impact from source-paper text alone without task-specific training. True-positive and false-positive rates, accuracy, and temporal consistency are reported for each LLM, and the models generalize reasonably well when trained on earlier cohorts and tested on later ones. Textual analysis of papers repeatedly labeled high-impact reveals a concentration of recurring methodological themes - most prominently causal inference and deep learning - suggesting that LLMs are sensitive to topical novelty. However, this approach does not report accuracy higher than the best task-specific models, the definition of "highly cited" varies enough between studies to make comparison difficult, and prediction performance depends critically on prompting details, choice of base LLM, and changes in scientific communication over time rather than on the papers themselves.

### 2.6 Lyutov et al. (2024): Interdisciplinarity and Journal Standards

Lyutov et al. (2024) examined whether the value of citations for interdisciplinary research depends on the standard of the journal in which a paper is published. They used data from the Scopus database covering 2,039 journals across 135 subject areas. Several machine-learning classifiers - Naive Bayes, Support Vector Machine (SVM), Logistic Regression, and Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP) - examined each paper's abstract as the primary source of information in order to determine its interdisciplinarity. The models attempted to classify a paper into its field of science according to its abstract, whereas the actual subject of the paper's journal was used as ground truth. Papers that could not be classified correctly were treated as interdisciplinary research papers, as their abstracts contained concepts related to several fields of science. Citation numbers of correctly and incorrectly classified papers were then compared across journal-quality groups. Interdisciplinary papers are not cited just because of their interdisciplinarity: the citation benefit of interdisciplinary papers is observable only in journals belonging to the top 10%, whereas elsewhere no citation advantage exists. This is visible in the shift in citation percentiles between correctly and incorrectly classified papers (Figure 5), with a clearly positive shift in the top 10% of journals (shaded region). This indicates that citation factors may not operate identically across venues, which must be taken into account when grouping journal features in our own prediction of citation ranges.

### 2.7 He et al. (2025): Dynamic Content-Aware Transformers for Citation Prediction

He et al. (2025) addressed the problem of predicting how many times a research paper will be cited as a function of future time. The authors point out that current methods treat a research publication as a "static document," whereas its interpretation can change over time as the research area evolves. To solve this, they proposed DICTA (Dynamic Content-aware Transformer), a deep-learning approach fusing two types of information: historical citation counts and the content of the paper's abstract. An LSTM-based dynamic content module learns the evolution of the abstract over time, and a Transformer-based encoder-decoder models the sequence of past citation counts to predict future counts. Tested on PubMed Central (PMC) and DBLP against machine-learning and deep-learning baselines, DICTA achieved RMSE values of 0.0431 (PMC) and 0.0253 (DBLP), improving over baselines by approximately 70% on average; ablations showed the dynamic content component was a significant contributor. The study demonstrates that both textual content and the temporal distribution of citations carry predictive information about future impact.

### 2.8 Fu and Aliferis (2008): Long-Term Prediction in Biomedical Research

Fu and Aliferis (2008) investigated whether the future citation impact of biomedical research papers could be predicted using information available at publication time. The authors developed computational models combining content-based and bibliometric features and evaluated their ability to predict citation counts over horizons of up to ten years. Four model types were compared: a complete model with all features, a content-only model, a bibliometric-only model, and a model based only on journal Impact Factor. Machine-learning and statistical techniques assessed the predictive contribution of each feature group. The complete models showed that citation impact can be predicted with considerable accuracy from publication-time information, with AUC values ranging from 0.857 to 0.918 depending on the citation threshold. Both content-based and bibliometric information contribute to prediction, while bibliometric characteristics such as author citation history and journal Impact Factor were particularly important. The study therefore provides evidence that citation impact is influenced by multiple characteristics rather than by scientific content alone. However, the study has several limitations relevant to the present project. Its dataset consists of biomedical publications, so findings may not generalize to computer science or other disciplines. The publication data are also from an older period (1991-1994), so modern factors such as social-media visibility, preprints, and contemporary open-access practices are not represented. Furthermore, variables such as author citation history and journal Impact Factor reflect existing academic reputation and visibility, meaning strong predictive performance does not establish causation. The study therefore demonstrates prediction rather than definitive causal relationships.

### 2.9 Zhang and Wu (2024): Predictability of Impact Across Research Areas

Zhang and Wu (2024) investigated the predictability of academic papers' impact in different research areas. The study was conducted on two large scholarly datasets, DBLP and arXiv, with millions of citations and papers. The authors propose a Multiple Models (MM) approach: papers are divided into sub-groups by research area and further by number of initial citations received. Titles, abstracts, references, relationships, research areas, publication information, and early citation counts were considered, and a prediction model was built for every area-and-citation-level group. The authors discovered significant differences in citations depending on field of research, concluding that citation behavior is not uniform across disciplines. The distribution of citations across research areas shows clear differences (figure 6.a). The study also examined papers at different citation levels and found distinct citation-growth trends over time for low-, medium-, and highly-cited papers (Figure 6.b). The proposed Multiple Models approach generally outperformed comparison approaches, especially for highly cited papers across research fields. The authors also found that when citation-history data were scarce, research area was more useful; and as more years of citation data were observed, early citation information became increasingly helpful. Overall, the study demonstrates how research attributes and citation history affect the accuracy of citation-impact prediction.

### 2.10 Bin Makhashen and Al-Jamimi (2023): Bibliometric Characteristics and Machine Learning

Bin Makhashen and Al-Jamimi (2023) explored whether highly cited papers (HCPs) can be detected by machine-learning algorithms based on bibliometric characteristics alone. The researchers extracted 20 features derived from paper, author, and journal data for 32,700 articles from Clarivate/Web of Science published between 2009 and 2022. These features included number of authors, international cooperation, interdisciplinary cooperation, title length, abstract length, number of keywords, number of references, funding information, publication year, citation count, paper usage, journal volume and issue, number of research areas, and open access. Articles were categorized into two groups (highly cited vs regularly cited), with HCPs defined as the top 1% by citation count within their field of science. The authors experimented with Logistic Regression, Decision Tree, KNN, SVM, Random Forest, MLP, model fusion, and LSTM. Random Forest was the most accurate algorithm at 97.9% accuracy. The authors also noted that highly cited articles differ from regular articles in that they are cited in a roughly linear fashion, and feature analysis showed HCPs have more authors, citations, and recent usage than regular articles (figure 7). Therefore, bibliometric feature sets appear useful for identifying highly cited research papers - though the extreme class imbalance inherent in a top-1% target makes raw accuracy difficult to interpret, a caveat we revisit when comparing our own results.

### 2.11 Mammola et al. (2021): Reference-List Characteristics and Citation Success

Mammola et al. (2021) investigated whether characteristics of a paper's reference list are associated with the number of citations it subsequently receives. The authors analyzed 50,878 research articles published in 17 ecological journals between 1997 and 2017. Seven numerical reference-list characteristics were extracted: reference-list size, self-citations, proportion of recent references, impact factor of cited journals, proportion of non-journal references, temporal span of the reference list, and proportion of seminal papers. Regression-based analyses, including linear mixed-effects models, examined relationships between reference-list characteristics and citation accumulation while accounting for variation among journals. Highly cited papers generally had longer reference lists and cited more recent and higher-impact references, and contained more self-citations. More specifically, citation counts increased significantly with reference-list length, proportion of self-citations, proportion of recent references, and total impact factor of the references. In contrast, proportion of non-journal publications, temporal span of references, and proportion of cited seminal papers showed no significant effects. Referencing practices therefore appear to be an important factor associated with citation impact, although the authors emphasize results should be interpreted within ecology. A major limitation is the restriction to 17 ecological journals, so findings may not transfer to disciplines with different referencing practices. The study focuses specifically on reference-list characteristics and thus cannot explain other potential factors such as author reputation, article content, journal prestige, accessibility, or topic popularity. The authors themselves describe their conclusions as a "recipe" for impactful reference lists in ecology, underscoring the field-specific nature of the findings.

### 2.12 Abrishami and Aliakbary (2019): Sequential Prediction via Early Citation History

Abrishami and Aliakbary (2019) examined whether future citation impact can be predicted from a paper's early citation history. Rather than relying primarily on paper characteristics such as author reputation, journal quality, or textual content, the study treats citation accumulation as a sequential prediction problem. Artificial neural-network approaches, including Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs) and sequence-to-sequence models, learn citation patterns from the first few years following publication and predict longer-term citation counts. The proposed models were evaluated against existing state-of-the-art citation-prediction methods. The deep-learning approach outperformed the compared methods in predicting both yearly and total future citation counts, indicating that a paper's early citation trajectory contains substantial information about its subsequent impact, and that citation accumulation is not completely random. However, the approach focuses primarily on the citation sequence itself rather than explaining the underlying characteristics that initially cause a paper to receive citations; author reputation, journal characteristics, research topic, and paper content are not comprehensively examined. In addition, reliance on an older publication period may limit generalizability to modern scholarly communication.

## 4 Research Gap

Across the twelve studies reviewed, three recurring limitations emerge. First, the field is fragmented by signal type. Content-based studies (Vital Jr. et al., 2024; Ye et al., 2026) deliberately exclude author, venue, and institutional signals, whereas bibliometric studies (Zhu et al., 2025; Bin Makhashen & Al-Jamimi, 2023; Mammola et al., 2021) exclude what the paper actually says. Only Fu and Aliferis (2008) compared complete, content-only, and bibliometric-only models head-to-head - and that comparison was limited to 3,788 biomedical articles from the early 1990s. Second, most work is confined to a single domain, journal, or database, and the definition of "highly cited" (top 1% vs top 20% vs absolute thresholds) and its normalization base (journal vs field vs year) differ across studies, making reported accuracies difficult to compare. Third, evaluation is typically static: models are trained and tested over overlapping periods, so measured performance partly reflects fitting the same era rather than genuinely forecasting future impact.

This project addresses these gaps by building an integrated, interpretable model on a large multi-venue corpus (AMiner dblp-v10; ~100,000 papers, 2005-2014) that combines content features (TF-IDF and transformer/SBERT embeddings), bibliometric exposure features (venue prestige proxies, team size, reference-list characteristics), and a strictly temporal evaluation protocol (train 2005-2012, test 2013-2014). Ablation experiments mirror Fu and Aliferis (2008) by comparing content-only, bibliometric-only, and complete models on identical data, and sensitivity analyses test robustness across multiple "highly cited" thresholds and against a known citation-count imputation artifact in the data.
